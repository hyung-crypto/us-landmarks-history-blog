The Statue of Liberty: The Gift That Almost Never Arrived

She was supposed to be finished for America's 100th birthday. She was three years late, funded by pennies from schoolchildren, and nearly rejected by the very city that now can't imagine its skyline without her.

By Chloe Bennett · Icons & Landmarks Correspondent

New York Harbor, 1886. A ship's horn cuts through the fog, and for the first time, a 305-foot copper woman raises her torch over the water — not as decoration, but as a promise. Did you know the Statue of Liberty was originally a rich copper-brown color, not the sea-green we know today? It took about 20 years of oxidation to turn her that iconic patina.

A Promise Built on Pennies

The idea began as a French political statement about liberty and republicanism, championed by French jurist Édouard René de Laboulaye. But there was a catch: France would build the statue — America had to build the pedestal. And by 1885, American fundraising had stalled so badly that construction nearly stopped entirely.

Did You Know #1 Publisher Joseph Pulitzer rallied the public through his newspaper New York World, promising to print the name of every donor — no matter how small. More than 120,000 people contributed, many sending just a few cents, and the pedestal fund was finally completed.

"Give Me Your Tired, Your Poor"

In 1883, poet Emma Lazarus wrote a sonnet to help raise pedestal funds. It was largely ignored during her lifetime — but in 1903, a bronze plaque bearing her words was mounted inside the pedestal, forever linking the statue to the story of immigration. For millions arriving through nearby Ellis Island, she was the first glimpse of a new life.

Did You Know #2 Bartholdi's mother is often rumored to have been his model for the statue's face — though historians debate this. What's certain: her seven-spiked crown represents the seven continents and seven seas, symbolizing universal liberty.

🧭 Traveler's Perspective

  • Best time to visit: Early morning ferry (first departure) avoids both crowds and harsh midday light for photos.
  • Best photo spot: The Battery Park promenade at sunset gives you the statue silhouetted against Manhattan's skyline.
  • Crown access: Requires a separate reservation booked months in advance — plan ahead if you want to climb inside.
  • Nearby: Combine with Ellis Island (same ferry ticket) for the full immigration-history experience.
  • Getting there: Ferries depart from Battery Park (NYC) and Liberty State Park (NJ) — book directly through Statue Cruises to avoid third-party markups.

From Copper Woman to Cultural Icon

Today, the statue is one of the most photographed monuments on Earth — but its meaning has never stopped evolving. She has stood through two World Wars, welcomed generations of immigrants, and become shorthand, worldwide, for the very idea of freedom.

Did You Know #3 The original torch was replaced in 1986 — the current one is covered in 24k gold leaf and lit at night, while the 1886 original torch is now on permanent display in the museum at the base.

She was never meant to be a tourist attraction. She was meant to be a message — and more than a century later, ships still pass beneath her torch every single day.
