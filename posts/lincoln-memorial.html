Lincoln Memorial: The Temple Built for a Man Who Wanted a Log Cabin

Critics said a Greek temple was too grand for a president who split rails as a boy. A century later, that same temple became the backdrop for "I Have a Dream" — and the place a nation kept returning to whenever it needed to say something it couldn't say anywhere else.

When Congress finally agreed to build a memorial to Abraham Lincoln, the argument wasn't over whether he deserved one — it was over what kind of building could possibly suit him. Architect Henry Bacon proposed a Greek temple. Many thought that was absurd for a man remembered for humble beginnings, and pushed instead for a simple log cabin shrine. The commission held firm, and the marble temple we know today rose instead on reclaimed, swampy land in West Potomac Park that critics considered too remote to matter.

Built on Reclaimed Land, Against Its Critics

Ground broke in February 1914, and the project took eight years and roughly $300,000 to complete. The statue of Lincoln inside was originally planned at just 10 feet tall — but as the vast interior chamber took shape, the sculptors realized a figure that size would look lost inside it. Daniel Chester French enlarged it to 19 feet, supervising six members of the Piccirilli family as they carved it from 28 separate blocks of Georgia marble.

Did You Know #1 The memorial's 36 exterior columns represent the 36 states in the Union at the time of Lincoln's assassination — while the frieze above lists all 48 states that existed by the memorial's 1922 dedication.

The Stage the Nation Kept Choosing

Long before Martin Luther King Jr. stood on its steps, the Lincoln Memorial had already become something more than a monument. In 1939, when the Daughters of the American Revolution refused to let contralto Marian Anderson perform for an integrated audience at Constitution Hall, Eleanor Roosevelt helped arrange an alternative: a free outdoor concert on the memorial's steps. Seventy-five thousand people came in person, and a national radio audience heard it live.

Then, on August 28, 1963, roughly 250,000 people gathered on the grounds for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. Organizer Walter Reuther had pushed for the location specifically — believing it would feel less confrontational to Congress than a rally at the Capitol, and more resonant beneath Lincoln's gaze. It was there that King delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech, deliberately echoing the language of the Gettysburg Address inscribed just steps away.

Did You Know #2 An enduring legend claims Robert E. Lee's face is hidden in the carving on the back of Lincoln's head, gazing across the Potomac toward Arlington. The National Park Service has confirmed this is a myth — but it hasn't stopped visitors from looking.

🧭 Traveler's Perspective

  • Best time to visit: Early morning or just after sunset, when the crowds thin and the statue's expression softens under artificial light.
  • Quiet moment: Stand on the 18th step down from the statue, engraved to mark the exact spot where King delivered "I Have a Dream."
  • Best photo spot: The Reflecting Pool at the base of the steps captures the full temple front and, on calm evenings, its mirror image.
  • Nearby: The Vietnam Veterans Memorial and Korean War Veterans Memorial are both a short walk away, on the same stretch of the Mall.
  • Accessibility: An elevator was added in the 1970s for visitors unable to use the 58 interior steps.

Still Being Written

Did You Know #3 The memorial has appeared on the back of the U.S. five-dollar bill since 1929, and from 1959 to 2008 it appeared on the reverse of the one-cent coin — meaning Lincoln's face and his memorial once shared the same coin.

A century after its dedication, the Lincoln Memorial is still being expanded — a $69 million project is transforming the long-closed undercroft beneath the chamber into a public museum and theater. But the building's real permanence was never architectural. It's that whenever the country has needed a place to gather, grieve, or demand something better of itself, it has kept walking back to the same marble steps.
